Who Was Thekla?

Saint Thekla (also spelled Thecla) is one of the most beloved women of the early Christian world — a disciple of Saint Paul, a model of holiness and courage, and a symbol of God's protection over those who surrender their lives to Him. Her story comes primarily from The Acts of Paul and Thekla, a 2nd-century Christian text that circulated widely among believers, especially in Syria, Anatolia, Egypt, and Greece.

Her life inspired generations of women to dedicate themselves to Christ, and she is still honored today in the Eastern Orthodox Church, Oriental Orthodox Churches, many Eastern Catholic Churches, and by believers around the world who see in her a pattern of bold faith.

The Early Life of Thekla

According to the earliest versions of her story, Thekla was a young woman of Iconium (modern-day Konya, Turkey). She lived in a respectable household and was betrothed to a wealthy, influential man. Her life changed forever when she overheard the apostle Paul preaching the Gospel in a house nearby.

For three days, Thekla sat by a window, refusing food, drink, or conversation — absorbed entirely by the message of Christ. Paul's teaching on:

  • purity of heart,
  • devotion to God,
  • resurrection,
  • and the new creation in Christ

ignited something deep within her. She chose Jesus with all her heart, knowing full well this would cost her everything.

Persecution and the First Trial: The Fire

Thekla's family and fiancée were enraged. She was dragged before the governor, accused of dishonoring her betrothal, and condemned to death by burning.

But God intervened.

As the flames rose around her, a sudden storm broke open above the arena. Thunder, rain, and divine fire extinguished the flames, saving her unharmed. The earliest manuscripts differ slightly:

  • Some say a miraculous cloud descended.
  • Others say a heavenly rainstorm put out the fire.
  • Others describe a protective wall of light around her.

But all traditions agree: God preserved her life.

Thekla's Companion: The Lioness

After escaping Iconium, Thekla continued to follow Paul. She traveled to Antioch, where a powerful local noble attempted to assault her, and she resisted him boldly. Enraged, he brought charges against her and had her condemned once more to death — this time in the arena with wild beasts.

It was here that Thekla encountered one of the most cherished elements of her story:

A lioness was brought to the arena — and instead of harming Thekla, she became her protector.

The Lioness Saved Thekla From:

1. A Bear

When a massive bear charged toward Thekla, the lioness attacked and killed the bear before it reached her.

2. A Male Lion

The next threat was a powerful male lion. The lioness fought the lion head-on, and both animals died in the struggle — giving her life to protect Thekla.

This scene has been cherished especially in the Eastern traditions, where it symbolizes:

  • God sending strength where the world expects weakness
  • The honoring of Thekla's purity, courage, and total devotion
  • Divine protection over women who face violence

The lioness and Thekla became symbols of holy sisterhood, strength, and divine deliverance.

The Water Trial and the Attempted Violation

One version of the story reports another cruel sentence: that Thekla would be thrown into a pool filled with aggressive seals or other aquatic beasts, a practice sometimes described in Roman amphitheaters.

Thekla cried out, declaring that she was baptizing herself in the name of Jesus Christ.

Before the animals could harm her:

  • lightning struck the water,
  • the beasts died,
  • and Thekla emerged alive — symbolically baptized and divinely protected.

The Miracle of the Cleft Rock

After many trials, Thekla continued to preach and heal in Seleucia (near modern Mersin, Turkey) for decades. Her ministry drew many women to Christ, especially those who suffered oppression or violence.

But even in her old age, her enemies pursued her.

According to the most ancient tradition, when a group of men attempted to overpower her — intending to violate and kill her — Thekla cried out to God:

"O Lord, God of heaven and earth,
You who have been my helper in every trial,
do not abandon me now."

In response, the mountain behind her split open, forming a narrow cleft.

Thekla entered the rock, and when she was safely inside, the mountain closed behind her, sealing her away from danger.

Some versions say this was the moment she entered eternal life. Others say the cleft remains a holy place where she continued to heal the sick. To this day, pilgrims visit the Tekla cave-shrine in Turkey, honoring the miracle.

Was Thekla a Real Historical Person?

Historians are divided, but several points are widely recognized:

  1. The Acts of Paul and Thekla is a 2nd-century text
    It is not Scripture, but it was extremely popular among early Christians.
  2. Thekla may have been based on a real follower of Paul
    Many scholars believe a real woman named Thekla existed and was remembered through oral tradition.
  3. Her cult was ancient and widespread
    Archaeology shows that Thekla's veneration was not fringe — it was universal across the early church in the East.
  4. Churches, shrines, and monasteries dedicated to her are ancient
    Her shrine in Seleucia was one of the largest pilgrimage centers in the early Christian world.

Whether every detail of the legend is literal or symbolic, Thekla's impact is unquestionable.

Which Churches Recognize or Venerate Thekla?

Eastern Orthodox Church

The Eastern Orthodox Church fully venerates her as:

  • Πρωτομάρτυς — Protomartyr among women
  • Ἰσαπόστολος — Equal-to-the-Apostles

Her feast days vary slightly by region (usually September 24), and countless monasteries, churches, and shrines across Greece, Turkey, Syria, Lebanon, and Egypt have been dedicated to her.

Oriental Orthodox Churches

The Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, and Ethiopian Churches also honor Thekla deeply. In the Syriac tradition, she is often called:

  • Thekla the Purified
  • Bride of the Unfading Light

The Coptic Synaxarium includes her feast and recounts her miracles.

Eastern Catholic Churches

Byzantine (Melkite, Ukrainian, Romanian, etc.) and Syriac Catholic Churches celebrate her feast because they preserve the Eastern hagiographical tradition.

Roman Catholic Church

Rome does not canonize her formally in the modern sense, but:

  • She is present in the ancient Martyrologium Romanum (older editions).
  • She appears in medieval Western calendars.
  • Many Catholic scholars acknowledge her as a saintly figure.
  • Her shrines were visited by Western pilgrims for centuries.

So while she is not officially "St. Thekla" in the contemporary Roman canon, she is honored historically.

Protestant Traditions

Many Protestants do not formally venerate saints, but Thekla is respected as:

  • A historical example of early female discipleship
  • A model of purity, courage, and evangelistic zeal
  • A symbol of God's protection over women

Evangelical, Anglican, and Lutheran scholars often cite her as an early witness of Christian womanhood.

Variations in Her Story

Because Thekla's story was told across cultures, a few variations exist:

1. The Trials in the Arena

All versions agree the lioness protected her, but:

  • Some manuscripts add wolves
  • Others add a bull or ox
  • The "water trial" involving seals appears in certain Greek texts
  • Syriac versions emphasize her prayers and hymns during the trials

2. Her Relationship With Paul

The earliest text makes their relationship spiritual, not romantic. Later legends sometimes:

  • Emphasize her role as Paul's "daughter in the faith"
  • Claim she taught alongside him
  • Describe her as the first female evangelist

3. Her End

There are three main endings:

  • The cleft rock closes around her, and she enters eternal life.
  • She lives to extreme old age in Seleucia, healing and teaching.
  • She disappears into the rock but continues her ministry invisibly, similar to Elijah traditions.

4. Her Iconography

Depending on region, she may be shown:

  • With two lions (symbolic)
  • With one lioness (literal)
  • In purple robes (royal dignity in Christ)
  • With a cross or scroll (teacher and witness)
  • Emerging from the split rock

The lioness is a particularly beloved detail in the Greek and Syriac traditions.

What Thekla Means to Us Today

Saint Thekla stands as a model for:

  • Courageous Christian womanhood
  • Purity and devotion to Christ
  • Holy resistance in the face of injustice
  • Spiritual authority grounded in humility
  • Endurance through trials
  • God's miraculous protection

Her life shows that Christ calls women not only to holiness but to boldness — to preach, to teach, to heal, and to stand firm when the world rages.

The lioness, the storms, the fire, the water, and the cleft rock all testify to the same truth:

  • God defends His daughters.
  • God strengthens the meek.
  • God calls women into ministry.

For the Order of Thekla, she is not merely a historic figure — she is a patron, a sister, and a spiritual companion who shows us the way of fearless love.
